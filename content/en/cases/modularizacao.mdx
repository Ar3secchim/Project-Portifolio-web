## Context

On the same Cobrix (fictional name) collections system, the cost of small code changes kept growing: a change in one module still required understanding effects on three others.

## Constraints

- External contracts (public API, published events) had to stay compatible through the entire migration.
- The team needed to keep shipping new functionality during the restructuring — there was no pause dedicated to refactoring.
- The new architecture had to be automatically verifiable, not just described in a decision document.

## Architecture

Modules were organized by business capability (collections, disputes, reporting), each with a clear boundary: dependencies between modules can only point to explicitly published interfaces, never internal classes. Architecture tests verify these boundaries on every build, alongside the functional suite.

## What did not work

Moving files into new packages without changing a single import gave the appearance of modularization without changing anything real — the dependency cycles were still there, just hidden behind nicer folder names. Real progress started when the cycles were measured and broken one flow at a time.

## Main lesson

Modularization is not a folder structure. It is the ability to prove, with a test, who can depend on whom — and why.
